Emotional Contagion Test Ethics

Introduction

In 2012, “emotional contagion” was a well-documented phenomenon where people would transfer positive or negative emotions to each other through in-person interactions. As social media was becoming more mainstream, in January 2012, Facebook was interested in if emotional contagion could also be spread through news feeds on their platform.

To investigate this, Facebook randomly selected 689,003 users, split them into treatment and control groups, and omitted a large amount of posts with positive or negative words on the news feeds for those in the treatment groups. Facebook omitted a similar amount of posts in the news feeds for those in the control group, but omitted posts randomly, without considering if the post contained positive or negative words.

For one week in January 2012, Facebook researchers recorded every word these users posted, and calculated the percentage that were positive or negative. At the end of the experiment, they found that those who had positive content on their feed reduced had more negative emotional expression compared to their control group, and those who had negative content on their feed reduced had more positive emotional expression compared to their control group. Both of these experiments had p-values of P < 0.001, providing strong evidence that emotional contagion could be spread online via the content someone sees. Facebook researchers compiled this information and published these findings in the PNAS scientific journal in July 2014.

Thus, this study involved gathering data on 689,003 users to determine how content someone sees influences their emotional expression. Though this type of study is helpful, as Facebook should be aware of how its content affects its users, one could be concerned about whether this data was collected ethically, and how Facebook has been using the data since.

Was informed consent possible?

The day after the article was published, PNAS published an Editorial Expression of Concern about potential personal privacy issues. The journal explained that it is generally best practice to make sure participants have given informed consent to participate in scientific research based on the “Common Rule” set by the US Department of Health and Human Services Policy for the Protection of Human Research Subject (2). It is not right to experiment on people who have not agreed to be tested on.

However, PNAS stated that “as a private company Facebook was under no obligation to conform to the provisions of the Common Rule when it collected the data used by the authors” (2). The journal also cited the original authors’ claim that the experiment was consistent with Facebook’s Data Use Policy, which all users agreed to when creating an account.

According to an article from The Guardian published July 2012, Facebook’s Data Use Policy did have a section on how they “may use the information we receive about you” prior to the study being conducted in January 2012. This section had examples of how data might be used, however, “research” was only explicitly mentioned in this section four months after the study concluded (before the research paper was published) (3). Thus, if one believes that a user must explicitly consent to having their data used for research purposes, this would not include users in the study who had agreed to the previous Data Use Policy.

Furthermore, a study by the Pew Research Center found that only 9% of American adults always read privacy policy before agreeing, and a majority of Americans self-report very little to no understanding of data protection laws (4). Thus, even if research were explicitly mentioned, though Facebook might be able to argue in court that they did nothing wrong, ethics should not be built on technicalities, and the fact that users’ “informed consent” is derived from them clicking a button “agreeing” to terms and conditions they likely did not read futher highlights the dubious nature of users’ “informed consent.”

Who was measured?

If Facebook were interested in determining how emotional contagion spread through its active users, it should make sure that the experiment was representative of all of them. Experiments should also make sure that everyone measured is not harmed by the study, and take precautions to protect those who could be at greater risk of harm.

Based on the article published by the Facebook researchers, out of all Facebook users, the 689,003 users were randomly selected for participation by their User IDs. They were also randomly split into four groups for the two experiments. There were roughly 155,000 users in each group who had posted at least one status that week, meaning that the only possible selection bias in this study came from the fact that not everyone who was selected posted any status updates, meaning that emotional contagion was only tracked for people who actively post on Facebook (about 89% of those selected) (1). However, I believe the only way to increase the number of selected users who post would be to have some kind of filtering system to exclude inactive accounts. Since this would make selection not completely random, it could introduce a different kind of bias. Also, those who do not post on Facebook may not be active users, and thus are not be the primary target of this study. Thus, I think randomly selecting from all users and omitting those who did not post is a great way to ensure everyone who posts on Facebook is represented in the results.

Though great for representation, I am not sure if everyone should have been included in this study. Some people have health conditions such that their emotional states are dramatically affected by online content. Thus, if one of these individuals were included in the study, seeing negative content could have seriously harmed them.

Since this study was aimed at Facebook users at large, I do not believe omitting these users would have severely reduced the claims Facebook could make. If Facebook were specifically interested in users who are especially vulnerable to being harmed by seeing negative online content, a study designed specifically for them with guardrails in place to ensure that their specific needs are accounted for would make more sense.

Were the data made publicly available?

Frequently with online data, people are concerned about published experiments leaking sensitive personal information. Though studies aim to answer a research question for the public, the people tested in the study deserve privacy, and should not suffer from people targeting them due to information released by the study.

The data collected in this experiment was the percentage of words users posted that were positive or negative. According to the original paper “no text was seen by the researchers” (1). The paper did not publish individual users’ percentage of positive or negative words. The paper only included graphs comparing the average percentage of words that were positive or negative for each of the four groups. Thus, identifiable data that could be used to determine an individual’s tendency to be affected by emotional online content was never released to the public by this study. The ethical concerns with this data collection lie in the process of collecting the data, not the privacy concerns of release of it.

However, the general story that the research told showed how humans are susceptible to emotional manipulation by algorithms, and was made clear to the public when Facebook released their paper. So though individuals could not be targeted as a result of the data Facebook released, someone intending to do harm may have been able to come up with new ways to target people as a collective based on this paper’s findings.

Is the data being used in unintended ways to the original study?

Though a study could intend to do good by releasing a data set, they should be cognizant of how bad actors could use their data to do harm.

In Facebook’s emotional contagion case, it is unclear what their original intentions were. Given the lack of well-researched information about how social media platforms affected users in 2012, one generous interpretation is that Facebook was interested in how its users were affected so that the platform could be conscious of its power and identify how best to curate a user’s feed to avoid doing harm.

If this were a reasonable interpretation in 2012, since then, this idea of manipulating users through online content has grown to inspire a profit-driven, undeniably exploitative algorithm that uses individuals’ data to curate their feeds and keep them on the site as long as possible, even in spite of their well-being. When former Facebook employee Frances Haugen testified in the Senate in 2020, she spoke about this, and added that Facebook is aware its algorithm amplifies body insecurities in children and can lead to real-world violence (5). Though Facebook never claimed exactly what their intentions were with the original study, it is clear that since then, Meta has used what it has learned about its ability to affect its users for maximizing profit despite doing harm.

Thus, though we cannot claim whether data is being used in unintended ways, we can definitely claim it is not being used in ethical ways. I think this makes the concern over unintended applications is less relevant, since Meta themselves are collecting data for harmful purposes.

Conclusion

On the data science side, this study seemed to do effective investigative work. Facebook researchers successfully gathered data to answer a specific research question and included all users they hoped to gain insights about. From an ethical perspective, it seems Facebook did not take many precautions to ensure gathering this data would not cause harm. Furthermore, they seem to be using the information they received (and continue to receive through continuous similar experiments) primarily to maximize profits. Users do not seem to be Meta’s primary customers. Instead, user engagement is the commodity sold to advertisers. In 2012, this experiment was criticized by the public as “inherently dehumanizing,” (6) which seems to ring even more true now. Users are kept uninformed, Meta does experiments to learn more about them, and uses the results to profit at the user’s expense. Though this is not an easily defensible business model from an ethics perspective, Meta seems to continue to get away with this because their collection of our data has allowed them to create incredibly well-curated feeds capable of capturing a wide range of people potentially against their true wishes. We should take these studies as a cautionary reminder that the fact that data can be collected doesn’t necessarily mean it should be.
