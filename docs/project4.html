Emotional Contagion Test Ethics

Introduction

In 2012, “emotional contagion” was a well-documented phenomenon where people would transfer positive or negative emotions to each other through in-person interactions, without either person necessarily being aware of this transfer. As social media was becoming more mainstream, in January 2012, Facebook was interested in if emotional contagion could be spread through news feeds on their platform, without the need for in-person interactions.

To investigate this, Facebook ran an experiment to test how reducing either positive or negative content on someone’s news feed affected the content that they posted. Facebook randomly selected 689,003 users to run two parallel experiments. Half of the users were used for the first experiment. Half of this experiment’s users were in the treatment group, where a user’s friends’ posts with at least one positive word had a 90% chance of not showing up on the user’s feed, and a user’s friends’ posts with at least one negative word had a 10% chance of not showing up on the user’s feed. The other half were the control group, where a similar amount of posts were ommitted from the news feed regardless of whether they were deemed as positive or negative. In the second experiment, half of the second experiment’s users were in the treatment group, where a user’s friends’ posts with at least one negative word had a 90% chance of not showing up on the user’s feed, and a user’s friends’ posts with at least one positive word had a 10% chance of not showing up on the user’s feed. The other half were the control group where a similar number of posts were removed from the user’s news feed (each experiment needed its own control group because more posts were ommitted in the experiment where 90% of positive posts were ommitted for the treatment group).

For one week in January 2014, Facebook researchers recorded everything these users posted, and calculated the percentage of words that were positive or negative. At the end of the experimental, they found that those in the first experiment who had positive content on their feed reduced had more negative emotional expression compared to their control group, and those in the second experiment who had negative content on their feed reduced had more positive emotional expression compared to control group. Both of these changes had p-values of P < 0.001, providing strong evidence that emotional contagion could be spread online via the content someone sees. Facebook researchers compiled this information and published these findings in the PNAS scientific journal in July 2014.

Though this type of study is helpful, as Facebook should be aware of how its content affects its users, one could be concerned about whether this data was collected ethically.

Was informed consent possible?

The day after the article was published, PNAS published an Editorial Expression of Concern about potential personal privacy issues. The journal explained that though it is generally best practice to make sure participants have given informed consent to participate in scientific research based on the “Common Rule” set by the US Department of Health and Human Services Policy for the Protection of Human Research Subjects, they stated that “as a private company Facebook was under no obligation to conform to the provisions of the Common Rule when it collected the data used by the authors” (2). The journal also cited the original authors’ claim that the experiment was consistent with Facebook’s Data Use Policy, which all users agreed to when creating an account.

According to an article from The Guardian published July 2012, Facebook’s Data Use Policy did have a section on how they “may use the information we receive about you” prior to the study being conducted in January 2012. This section had examples of how data might be used, however, “research” was only explicitly mentioned in this section four months after the study concluded (before the research paper was published). Thus, if one believes that a user must explicitly consent to having their data used for research purposes, this would not include users in the study who had agreed to the previous Data Use Policy.

Furthermore, I think it’s a fair assumption that people do not generally read terms and conditions. (Though one might argue people should!) A study by the Pew Research Center found that only 9% of American adults always read privacy policy before agreeing, and a majority of Americans self-report very little to no understanding of data protection laws. Thus, even if research were explicitly mentioned, though Facebook might be able to argue in court that they did nothing wrong, ethics should not be built on technicalities, and the fact that users’ “informed consent” is derived from them clicking a button “agreeing” to terms and conditions they likely did not read provides further reasons to doubt this whether this data was collected ethically.

Who was measured?

Based on the article published by the Facebook researchers, out of all Facebook users, the 689,003 users were randomly selected for participation by their User IDs. They were also randomly split into four groups for the two experiments. There were roughly 155,000 users in each group who had posted at least one status that week, meaning that the only possible selection bias in this study came from the fact that not everyone who was selected posted any status updates, meaning that emotional contagion was only tracked for people who actively post on Facebook (about 89% of those selected). However, I believe the only way to increase the number of selected users who post would be to have some kind of filtering system to exclude inactive accounts. Since this would make selection not completely random, it could introduce a different kind of bias. Also, those who do not post on Facebook are likely not the primary interest of this study. Thus, I think this way of randomly selecting users and needing to omit those who did not post is a great way to ensure everyone who posts on Facebook is represented in the results.

Though great for representation, I am not sure if everyone should have been included in this study due to the harms it could have caused them. Some people have health conditions such that their emotional states are dramatically affected by online content. Thus, if one of these individuals were included in the study, seeing negative content could have harmed them more than the average person. Though Facebook may have been interested in collecting their data in hopes of learning how to protect these individuals in the future, it seems like no precautions were put in place to ensure that doing this study wouldn’t hurt those with mood disorders, minors, or anyone else especially vulnerable to manipulation by this study.
